## Q1
(a) Define a project and explain uniqueness, temporariness and progressive elaboration as stated in project management standards. (b) Give at least four differences between projects and routine operations, and evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of managing work as a project. (c) Scenario: you oversee replacing an ageing electrical distribution board at a major port, needing coordination of safety, procurement and shutdown scheduling. Explain why this must be treated as a project, not routine maintenance, and the risks of misclassifying it.

::: answer
**(a)** A **project** is "a **temporary** endeavour undertaken to create a **unique** product, service or result" (PMI). ISO 10006: a unique process of coordinated and controlled activities with start and finish dates, to achieve an objective within constraints of time, cost and resources.

- **Uniqueness:** the output has never been produced in exactly this form: a different site, client, design, conditions or constraints, even if similar work has been done before.
- **Temporariness:** a **definite start and finish**. It ends when the objectives are met, or when it is terminated because they cannot be met or are no longer needed. (The product itself may last for decades.)
- **Progressive elaboration** *(beyond the notes)*: the project is defined **in more detail as it proceeds**. A concept note becomes a feasibility report, then a DPR with drawings, specifications and cost estimates. You cannot know every detail at the start.

**(b)** Projects vs operations:

| Project | Routine operation |
|---|---|
| Temporary: start and finish | Ongoing, repetitive |
| Unique output | Same output each time |
| Temporary, cross-functional team | Permanent functional staff |
| High risk and uncertainty | Low, known risk |
| Aims to **change** something (new asset) | Aims to **sustain** the business |
| Own budget, fixed scope | Annual operating budget |

*Advantages* of running work as a project: clear objectives and accountability (one project manager); integrated planning and control of time, cost and scope; cross-functional coordination; better risk management; handles complex, costly, risky work (the notes' benefits of the project approach).
*Disadvantages:* extra overhead (planning, reporting, documentation); temporary teams may conflict with line managers over people; can be too heavy for small routine jobs; knowledge may be lost when the team disbands.

**(c)** It is a **project** because it is a **one-off, unique** job with a **definite start and end**; it needs **cross-functional coordination** (electrical, safety, procurement, port operations); it carries **high risk** (live switchgear, arc flash, supply interruption to cranes and reefers); it needs a **planned shutdown** window agreed with port operations, **procurement** of a new board with lead time, and a **budget**. Routine maintenance (inspection, tightening, cleaning) has none of these.

*Risks of misclassifying it as maintenance:* no proper schedule, so the shutdown overruns and **port operations stop** (large financial loss); the board is **not ordered in time**; **safety** procedures (isolation, permit-to-work, lock-out/tag-out) are skipped, risking injury; poor **budget control** and no clear accountability; stakeholders are not informed.
:::

## Q2
(a) Describe the six key characteristics of a project. (b) Compare the importance and limitations of each in engineering projects, and give at least two disadvantages of misunderstanding them. (c) Scenario: a solar mini-grid team says its work is "routine" because it repeats similar installations in many villages. Is this valid, and what are the managerial consequences of accepting the claim?

::: answer
**(a)** From the notes:

1. **Unique in nature**: no two projects are identical.
2. **Definite objectives (goals)** to achieve.
3. **Requires a set of resources**: people, money, materials, equipment.
4. **Specific time frame**, with a definite start and finish.
5. **Involves risk and uncertainty.**
6. **Requires cross-functional teams** and an interdisciplinary approach.

**(b)**

| Characteristic | Importance in engineering | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Uniqueness | Each site needs its own design and risk review | Hard to reuse estimates exactly |
| Specific objectives | Gives clear, measurable targets (e.g. 50 kWp, 99% availability) | Objectives may change (scope creep) |
| Time frame | Allows scheduling and deadlines | Fixed deadlines can pressure quality and safety |
| Resources | Drives budgeting and procurement | Resources are limited and shared |
| Cross-functional | Brings civil, electrical and procurement skills together | Coordination is difficult; conflicts |
| Uncertainty | Forces risk analysis and contingency | Cannot be removed, only managed |

*Disadvantages when misunderstood:* (1) **under-planning**: no schedule, budget or risk plan, leading to time and cost overruns; (2) **unclear objectives and responsibilities**, causing rework and disputes; (3) **ignored risks**, causing safety incidents or failures.

**(c)** The argument is **not valid**. The installations are *similar*, but each village is **unique**: different site, load profile, soil, community, permits and logistics. Each has a **defined start and finish**, **specific objectives**, its own **resources and budget**, **risks** (weather, theft, community acceptance, supply delays) and needs a **cross-functional** team. They are best managed as a **programme of repeated projects** that reuses standard designs, templates and lessons learned.

*Consequences of accepting the claim:* no site-specific surveys or designs (wrongly sized systems); no proper schedule or procurement plan; risks and stakeholder issues ignored; no cost control or accountability per site; poor quality and safety; lessons not captured.
:::

## Q3
(a) Define project scope, time and cost, and explain how they form the Quality Triangle. (b) Compare their interactions, with advantages of balancing them and risks of changing one abruptly. (c) Scenario: a 5-week project to install a 200 kVA transformer is halfway done when the client increases the capacity to 500 kVA. Analyse the effect on the three dimensions and propose a decision that protects project success.

::: answer
**(a)**

- **Scope:** all the work needed to deliver the product with the specified features (the specifications).
- **Time:** the schedule: start, duration and completion date.
- **Cost:** the budget: resources and money needed.

They are drawn as an **equilateral triangle**, the **Quality (project performance) triangle**: $\text{Performance} = f(\text{Scope}, \text{Cost}, \text{Time})$. A project succeeds when it meets its scope within time and budget, and changing one side changes the others.

**(b)** Interactions: more **scope** needs more **time** and **cost**; less **time** needs less scope or more cost (crashing); less **cost** needs less scope or more time.

*Advantages of balancing them:* realistic plans, predictable delivery, quality maintained, satisfied stakeholders, controlled risk.
*Risks of abrupt change:* schedule overrun, budget overrun, lower quality or safety, rework and waste, team stress, contract disputes.

**(c)** Effect of changing from 200 kVA to 500 kVA halfway:

- **Scope:** much larger: a bigger transformer, plinth and foundation, larger HV and LV cables, switchgear, protection settings, earthing, possibly a new utility connection approval. Some completed work (plinth, cables) may have to be **redone**.
- **Time:** a 500 kVA unit has a longer **procurement lead time**; redesign and approvals add weeks; the 5-week finish will slip.
- **Cost:** a more expensive transformer and accessories, rework, extra labour and delay costs.

**Decision:** treat it as a formal **change request** through **change control**: stop affected work, assess the impact on scope, time and cost; prepare a revised design, schedule and budget; get the **client's written approval** of the new time and cost (a contract **variation**) before proceeding; re-baseline the plan and inform all stakeholders. If the client cannot accept the extra time and cost, complete the 200 kVA scope and handle 500 kVA as a separate phase.
:::

## Q4
(a) Explain the four phases of the project life cycle. (b) Differentiate between the S-curve and the J-curve patterns, with their advantages, disadvantages and typical uses. (c) Scenario: a desalination plant extension progresses very slowly for two months, then rapidly after bulk equipment deliveries. Which curve is this, and how should monitoring be adjusted?

::: answer
**(a)** From the notes:

1. **Conceptualisation (initiation):** identify the idea, pre-feasibility and feasibility, appraisal and approval. *Output:* concept note, feasibility report.
2. **Planning:** identify and sequence activities, time frame, estimates and budget, staffing. *Output:* Detailed Project Report.
3. **Execution:** plans are put into operation; communicate, review progress, monitor cost and time, control quality, manage changes.
4. **Termination:** deliverables installed and put into operation, follow-up and evaluation.

**(b)**

| | S-curve | J-curve |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Slow start, **fast middle**, slow finish | Slow start, then **ever faster** to the end |
| Typical use | Construction and infrastructure (e.g. watershed): planning and mobilisation at the start, handover and commissioning at the end | Projects where output accelerates once inputs are in place (e.g. energy plantation) |
| Advantage | Predictable; good for planning resources and cash flow | Captures late, rapid progress; little spent early |
| Disadvantage | Slow start and end can hide delays | Most progress comes late, so **risk is concentrated at the end**; early slowness is hard to judge |

**(c)** Slow progress for two months, then a sudden rapid rise is the **J-curve** pattern.

*Monitoring adjustments:* during the slow phase, monitor **leading indicators** (procurement milestones, approvals, delivery dates, factory tests) rather than percent complete; expedite suppliers. Before the deliveries, have **resources, cranes, storage and installation crews ready**. After the deliveries, increase the **review frequency** (e.g. weekly instead of fortnightly), track the critical path closely, and watch quality and safety during the rush.
:::

## Q5
(a) Define project management, project manager and project team, and link them to decision-making, planning and coordination. (b) Compare a structured project management approach with an informal one in engineering works. (c) Scenario: a contractor starts a railway signalling upgrade without a formal schedule; interference with train operations causes severe delays. Diagnose the root causes and propose corrective actions.

::: answer
**(a)**

- **Project management:** applying **knowledge, skills, tools and techniques** to project activities to meet the requirements; a distinct area of management with three features: a project manager, a project team and a **project management system** (organisation, information, decision-making and procedures for **integrated planning and control**).
- **Project manager:** the person **responsible for achieving the objectives**: plans, makes decisions, coordinates, communicates with stakeholders and controls time, cost and quality.
- **Project team:** the **cross-functional group** that carries out the work.

*Links:* the manager **decides** using information from the system; **planning** sets activities, sequence, schedule, budget and resources; **coordination** integrates the team, suppliers and stakeholders so the plan is executed and controlled.

**(b)**

| Structured approach | Informal approach |
|---|---|
| **Strengths:** clear scope, schedule (CPM) and budget; defined roles; risk and change control; progress measured against a baseline; suited to complex, risky work | **Strengths:** quick to start, flexible, little paperwork; fine for small, simple jobs |
| **Weaknesses:** more overhead and documentation; can be rigid | **Weaknesses:** no baseline to measure delay; poor coordination; risks unmanaged; frequent overruns and disputes; weak accountability |

**(c) Root causes:** no **formal schedule** or network, so no critical path and no coordination of work windows; no **stakeholder engagement** with the railway operator (possession times not agreed); no **risk assessment** of interference with live operations; no **resource or procurement plan**; no **monitoring and control** system to detect delays early.

**Corrective actions:** stop and **re-plan**: build the WBS, network and critical path; agree **track possessions** and work windows with the operator; do a **risk assessment** and contingency plan (night work, staged cut-overs, temporary signalling); assign clear responsibilities; set a **baseline** and review progress regularly; use a **change control** procedure; communicate with all stakeholders.
:::
