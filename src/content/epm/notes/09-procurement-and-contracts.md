# Procurement and contracts

*Chapter 4 · EEU 08106 Engineering Project Management*

## Procurement

**Procurement planning** decides **which project deliverables to buy in, and when**. Whenever goods or services are bought in, a **contract** is needed.

| Party | Also called |
|---|---|
| The organisation **supplying** goods or services | **Contractor**, **supplier** |
| The organisation **paying** for them | **Sponsor**, **owner**, **client**, **customer** |

### The 5 R's of procurement

Buy at the right:

1. **Place**
2. **Price**
3. **Quality**
4. **Quantity**
5. **Time**

### The procurement cycle (CTB, 2008)

The Central Tender Board cycle has **14 steps**; the board itself is involved in **steps 4 to 9**.

| Step | Activity |
|---|---|
| 1 | Determine needs, quantity, scope of works and terms of reference |
| 2 | Reconcile needs with funds |
| 3 | Prepare bid packages |
| **4** | **Choose the procurement method** |
| **5** | **Invite tenders** |
| **6** | **Receive tenders** |
| **7** | **Evaluate tenders and make recommendations** |
| **8** | **Select the supplier, contractor or consultant** |
| **9** | **Issue the letter of acceptance** |
| 10 | Finalise and execute the contract |
| 11 | Issue orders and instructions |
| 12 | Receive goods and monitor work |
| 13 | Make payment |
| 14 | Report on the performance of the contractor or consultant |

### Four simple steps of procurement

**Requirements planning → Solicitation → Award → Contract administration**

#### 1. Requirements planning

Defines the **content and boundary** of what is to be bought, as set out in the **project charter** or project definition report. It produces:

- the **Statement of Work (SOW)**;
- **specifications**: design, performance and functional; and
- the **Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)**.

#### 2. Solicitation

The process of **identifying a supplier**. It depends on the value of the purchase and the nature of the requirement:

- If the sponsor knows **what** it wants **and how**, it simply looks for the **best price**.
- If it knows **what** but not **how**, it asks for **proposals** on method, performance and price, then **negotiates**.

**Types of bid solicitation**

| | Request for Quotation (RFQ) | Request for Proposal (RFP) | Invitation to Tender (ITT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formality | **Informal**; often by fax or phone | Negotiated | **Most formal** |
| Document | One page: description of goods, terms and conditions | Detailed description of the project, intended results and **selection criteria** | Detailed specification of what is to be supplied |
| Response | Quotation with **price and delivery** | Proposal; bidders may later change price and technical details through **discussion** | Formal tender bid |
| Use | Survey the market for the **best price**, with no legal obligation | When the result can be achieved **in many ways**; **price is not the main factor** | **Higher-value** purchases; sent to any interested party; **tenders opened publicly** |

**The bid package** usually contains:

- the invitation to tender;
- general information to bidders;
- terms of reference or specifications;
- forms for the **bill of quantities** where needed; and
- the terms and conditions of the contract.

**Procurement methods:** public (open) tendering, selective tendering, sole (single-source) tendering, and the three-quote system.

**Single-stage vs two-stage tendering**

- **Single stage:** one shot. All information is available, a realistic price can be estimated, and the ITT is issued, evaluated, negotiated and awarded in one go.
- **Two stage:**
  1. The supplier is appointed **early, before the final design**, under a limited contract, so work can start early.
  2. When the specifications are final, a **fixed price** is agreed and the **full contract** issued.

#### Evaluating proposals

**Evaluation criteria** are used to rate or score bids. They may be limited to **purchase price** or include other factors. PMI (2013) lists these **source selection criteria**:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| **Understanding of need** | Does the proposal address the statement of work? |
| **Overall or life-cycle cost** | Will the seller give the lowest **total cost of ownership** (purchase + operating cost)? |
| **Technical capability** | Does the seller have, or can it get, the skills and knowledge? |
| **Risk** | How much risk is in the SOW, how much will the seller carry, and how will it be mitigated? |
| **Management approach** | Can the seller develop the processes to deliver a successful project? |
| **Technical approach** | Will the proposed methods and solutions meet the requirements? |
| **Warranty** | What will the seller warrant, and for how long? |
| **Financial capacity** | Does the seller have, or can it get, the money needed? |
| **Production capacity and interest** | Can and will the seller meet future requirements? |
| **Business size and type** | Does the seller meet a required category (e.g. small or disadvantaged business)? |
| **Past performance** | What is the past experience with this seller? |
| **References** | Can it provide references from previous customers? |
| **Intellectual property rights** | Does it claim IP rights in the work processes or products? |
| **Proprietary rights** | Does it claim proprietary rights in the work processes or products? |

**Evaluation methods**

- **Screening** of tenders (remove non-compliant bids).
- **Bid comparison** with a **weighted evaluation matrix**. Bidders' information (quoted price, delivery time, discounts) is tabulated on one summary form. **Technical evaluation** is done with the client's own engineers or experts, project manager or IT manager.
- **Demonstrations.**

> **Example: tender evaluation criteria with weights and pass marks**
>
> | Criterion | Weight | Pass mark |
> |---|---|---|
> | Project schedule / delivery plan | 30 | 60% |
> | Organisational experience | 15 | 60% |
> | Project staffing and resources | 15 | 50% |
> | Method statement and quality of proposal | 15 | 50% |
> | Financial capacity | 15 | 60% |
> | HSE (health, safety, environment) policy | 10 | 50% |
> | **Overall** | **100** | **60%** |

> **Example: weighted evaluation matrix (ITIL, 2012).** Each criterion is scored 10 (fully met), 7 (mostly), 3 (partly) or 0 (not usable), then multiplied by its weight; the weights add up to 100%. Vendor A scores **7.35** and vendor B **4.8**, so A wins. Price had the biggest single weight (20%), and vendor A won it 10 × 0.20 = 2.0 against 3 × 0.20 = 0.6.

#### 3. Award

**Final contractor selection**, ending in a **signed contract**. The aim is to negotiate a **contract type, payment method and price** that give the contractor a **reasonable risk** and the **greatest incentive to perform**.

#### 4. Contract administration

- **Contract change control:** the process for modifying the contract.
- **Performance reporting:** how well the contractor is meeting the contract objectives.
- Correspondence on terms and conditions, negotiating changes, **payment requests**.
- Warranties and waivers.
- **Breaches, delays, dispute resolution and arbitration**.
- **Force majeure** (events beyond either party's control).

## Contracts

### Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Target (estimated) cost** | The cost the contractor will most likely reach. |
| **Target (expected) profit** | The profit (fee) negotiated in the contract. |
| **Profit ceiling / floor** | Maximum and minimum profit. |
| **Price ceiling** | The maximum price. |
| **Maximum and minimum fee** | Fee limits, as percentages of the target cost. |
| **Sharing arrangement** | How a cost overrun or underrun is split between client and contractor, e.g. **85/15**. |
| **Point of total assumption (PTA)** | The cost or price above which the **contractor carries all** additional costs. |

**Price vs cost.** **Cost** is the contractor's expenses. **Price** is the total amount the client pays: cost **plus profit (fee)**.

### Types of contract

| Fixed price | Cost reimbursable | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|
| Firm fixed price (FFP) | Cost plus fixed fee (CPFF) | **Time and materials (T&M)** |
| Fixed price with economic price adjustment | Cost plus percentage fee | |
| Fixed price incentive fee (FPIF) | Cost plus incentive fee (CPIF) (predetermined formula) | |
| | Cost plus award fee (based on the client's satisfaction) | |

#### Fixed-price (lump-sum) contracts

- The contractor must **estimate the target cost carefully** and perform the work at the **negotiated price**.
- A profit is expected but **not guaranteed**; even at a loss, the contractor must perform.
- So the **contractor carries a large risk**, while the client has **little control** over how the work is done.
- Changes in requirements lead to **equitable adjustments** of the price.

#### Cost plus fixed fee (CPFF)

- Used when an **accurate price cannot be set**.
- Two elements: an **estimated cost** and a **fixed fee**. The contractor is reimbursed its costs and paid the fee on top.
- The **cost may vary but the fee stays the same**.
- The contractor's books must be **audited**.
- Used where **performance risk is high** and the client wants **a lot of control**.

#### Cost plus percentage fee

- The fee is a **percentage of the costs incurred**, so it **grows as spending grows**.
- It gives the client **no assurance of the final cost** (and no incentive for the contractor to save).

#### Cost plus incentive fee (CPIF)

- Like other cost-plus contracts, but the fee is **adjusted by a formula** comparing the **actual cost with the target cost**.
- The fee depends on how well the contractor meets performance objectives. It may include a fixed part plus an incentive part, usually with a **maximum** (and minimum) fee.

$$\text{Fee} = \text{Target fee} + \text{Contractor share} \times (\text{Target cost} - \text{Actual cost})$$

$$\text{Total price} = \text{Actual cost} + \text{Fee} \qquad (\text{Min fee} \le \text{Fee} \le \text{Max fee})$$

> **Example: CPIF contract.** Target cost 10 000, target fee 750, maximum fee 1 350, minimum fee 300, sharing 85/15 (client/contractor), actual cost 9 000.
>
> Underrun $= 10\,000 - 9\,000 = 1\,000$; the contractor's 15% share $= 150$
> $\text{Fee} = 750 + 150 = 900$ (between 300 and 1 350, so allowed)
> $\text{Total price} = 9\,000 + 900 =$ **9 900**
>
> The client saves 850 of the underrun (85%) and the contractor earns 150 more fee. An overrun works the other way: 1 000 over target would cut the fee to 600.

#### Time and materials (T&M) and labour hours

- A **hybrid** with features of both cost-reimbursable and fixed-price contracts.
- The contractor is paid for **hours of service** at a set **hourly rate**, whether or not the task is achieved, plus the **cost of materials** and a percentage fee.
- It is the **most expensive for the client** and carries the **least risk for the contractor**.

| Contract type | Risk to contractor | Risk to client |
|---|---|---|
| Firm fixed price | Highest | Lowest |
| Fixed price incentive | High | Low |
| Cost plus incentive fee | Medium | Medium |
| Cost plus fixed fee | Low | High |
| Cost plus percentage fee / T&M | Lowest | Highest |
