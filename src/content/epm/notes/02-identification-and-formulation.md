# Project identification and formulation

*Chapter 2 · EEU 08106 Engineering Project Management*

A project adds, directly or indirectly, to a nation's economy. Yet many major public projects, especially in irrigation, agriculture and infrastructure, suffer large **time and cost overruns**, and in the private sector the rise of sick industries and of banks' non-performing assets (NPAs) tells a similar story. The causes are **technical, financial, procedural and managerial**, and most of them stem from **inadequate project formulation and haphazard implementation**. Careful identification and formulation is the cure.

## Project identification

Projects are conceived to **meet market demand, exploit natural resources or create wealth**.

- **Development projects** come mainly from the **national planning process**.
- **Industrial projects** usually come from spotting **commercial prospects and profit potential**.

Because a project is a means to an end, several alternative projects may meet the same objective. A proposal should list the alternatives considered and justify the one chosen. Identification **screens the many project ideas** (from sectoral, opportunity and support studies, available data and expert opinion) down to a **few promising options**.

## Project formulation

> **Project formulation** is the process of presenting a project idea in a form in which it can be subjected to comparative appraisal, to decide in definite terms the priority it should get when resources are severely limited.

### Opportunity studies

An opportunity study **identifies investment opportunities**. It is normally done at **macro level** by economic planning and development agencies. There are three types:

1. **Area studies**
2. **Sectoral and sub-sectoral studies**
3. **Resource-based studies**

Opportunity and support studies give a sound basis for identifying projects.

### Pre-feasibility study

A pre-feasibility study sits **between the opportunity study and the full feasibility study**; the difference is mainly the **level of detail**. It gathers facts and opinions to decide **tentatively whether the idea is worth pursuing**.

- It assesses market potential, size of investment, technical feasibility, financial analysis and risk.
- Its breadth and depth depend on the time available and the decision maker's confidence.
- It produces a **project profile** for stakeholders and funding agencies.
- It highlights critical aspects that need deeper **functional support studies**.

**Support studies** are carried out before the pre-feasibility or feasibility study of large-investment projects, and also form part of the feasibility study. Each covers **one or more critical aspects** in detail, and its conclusions must be clear enough to guide the next stage.

### Feasibility study

The feasibility study is the **backbone of project formulation**. It investigates practicalities, ways of meeting the objectives, strategy options and methodology, and predicts the likely outcome, risks and consequences of each course of action. A good study gives a sound basis for decisions, clear objectives, logical planning, minimal risk and a cost-effective project.

Feasibility is judged against the **STEEP** factors:

| S | T | E | E | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Social | Technological | Ecological | Economic | Political |

A feasibility study is **not an end in itself**, only a means to an investment decision. It is made harder by the number of alternatives (technology, plant capacity, location, financing) and assumptions. It covers:

- economic and market analysis
- technical analysis
- financial analysis (see the next chapter)
- economic benefits
- project risk and uncertainty
- management aspects
- time frame for implementation

#### Economic and market analysis

Demand forecasts can **no longer be simple extrapolations of past trends**; many parameters influence the market and all likely developments must be considered. Many projects have failed **not for technical or financial reasons but because they ignored customer requirements and market forces**.

Market analysis looks at product specifications, pricing, distribution channels, trade practices, the threat of substitutes, domestic and international competition, and export opportunities, so the investment decision accounts for market risk.

#### Technical analysis

Technical analysis starts from the product description, specifications and quality standards. It compares the available technologies, selects the most appropriate one, and looks at how the technology will be acquired and licensed. The chosen technology must suit the raw materials and inputs available and give a **competitive production cost**.

| Aspect | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Technology** | Availability, alternatives, latest or state of the art, other implications |
| **Plant capacity** | Market demand, technological parameters |
| **Inputs** | Raw materials, components, power, water, fuel, others |
| **Other** | Skilled manpower, location, logistics, environmental considerations (pollution etc.), buildings and foundations |

#### Environmental impact studies

Almost every project affects the environment, and **environmental clearance** is needed for all projects. The environmental impact analysis is therefore done **before the feasibility study starts**. Its objectives are to:

1. **identify and describe** the environmental resources and values (ER/Vs), or environmental attributes, that the project will affect, quantified as far as possible;
2. **describe, measure and assess** the effects the project will have on them; and
3. **describe alternatives** that would achieve the same results with different environmental effects.

The study then specifies the equipment and facilities needed to comply with environmental regulations.

#### Economic benefits

Besides financial return, the study looks at **wider economic benefits**: employment, development of the project area, foreign-exchange savings (import substitutes) or earnings (exports).

#### Management aspects

The background of the promoters, management philosophy, organisation and staffing for the implementation and operating phases, decentralisation and delegation, systems and procedures, method of execution and **accountability**.

#### Time frame for implementation

The study gives a broad implementation time frame. It matters because it drives **pre-operative expenses and cost escalation**, which affect profitability.

#### Feasibility report

The findings are written up as the **techno-economic feasibility report** (project report), which is used for appraisal and the investment decision.

## Project appraisal

**Appraisal** is the **critical examination of the whole proposal**. It goes beyond the feasibility report, gathering extra information where needed, and ends with an **appraisal note** for the implementation decision.

| Appraisal | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Market** | Demand projections, marketing infrastructure, competence of key marketing staff |
| **Technical** | Product mix, capacity, manufacturing process, know-how and collaboration, raw materials, location and site, buildings, plant and equipment, manpower, **break-even point** |
| **Environmental** | Land use and micro-environment, use of natural resources, government policy |
| **Financial** | Capital cost, rate of return, contingencies, cost projections, capacity utilisation, financing pattern |
| **Economic** | Economic rate of return, effective rate of protection, domestic resource cost (a supporting appraisal) |
| **Managerial** | Promoters, organisation structure, managers, HR management |
| **Social cost–benefit (SCBA)** | Costs and benefits **to society**, which often differ from the money costs and revenues |

**Social cost–benefit analysis** follows one of two methods:

- **UNIDO method:** net benefits are valued at **economic (efficiency) prices**, also called **shadow prices**.
- **Little–Mirrlees (L–M) approach:** inputs and outputs are classed as (1) traded goods and services, (2) non-traded goods and services, and (3) labour.

Worldwide, the **economic rate of return (ERR)** based on SCBA now carries weight in investment decisions.

## Detailed Project Report (DPR)

Once the project is appraised and the investment approved, the **Detailed Project Report** is prepared. It holds all the relevant details, including **design drawings, specifications and detailed cost estimates**, and acts as the **blueprint for implementation**.
